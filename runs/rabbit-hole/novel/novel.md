# Twelve Words

## One: Blue

The payments file left White Hart House at ten every night and came back at seven every morning with its failures sorted into a queue, and Kieran Hale had cleared that queue for nine years. Returned item, account closed. Name mismatch. Sort code retired in 2011. Fraud rule forty-one. He could read an exception the way his grandfather Ron, a fitter in the railway works until they shut, had read a boiler by the sound it made: not the words on the screen but the shape of what had gone wrong behind them.

The Hart had been founded in 1868 by railwaymen pooling their wages in the back room of a White Hart inn so that each of them, one by one, could buy a house. It still had the brass hart on the door and a mural of the founders in the canteen, men with moustaches and bowler hats looking faintly surprised to find themselves on a wall in west Swindon. Kieran liked the mural. He liked most things about the Hart. That was the problem, if it was a problem.

His week had one shape. The 8.12 bus from Old Town. The lanyard. The tea round, which went clockwise from the window and which nobody had ever agreed to and everybody kept. Cut-off at ten. Lunch at one. The Thursday quiz at the Beehive, where his team came third with the regularity of a tide. Laura's rota on the fridge, printed monthly and held up by a magnet from Weymouth: days, nights, and the twelve-and-a-half-hour shifts she called longs. Sunday lunch in Penhill, where his father talked about the car plant closing in 2021 as if it were weather.

"They'll find something for the good ones," his father said, carving. "Pass the gravy."

"You're one of the good ones, Dad."

"Pass the gravy, Kieran."

He was twenty-nine. He was reliable. His appraisals used the word three times. At sixteen he had run a raiding guild of forty people across six countries in an online game, set the timetables, called the pulls, known who was sulking in Lisbon and who needed carrying in Tallinn, and nobody on the third floor knew it. When he took the job at twenty he had logged off for the last time, handed the guild to a Dutch boy called Sander, and become, deliberately, no trouble to anyone.

Some Sunday evenings the week sat on his chest like a cat. He did not mention it. It seemed an ungrateful thing to feel in a town where people had queued outside the plant gates for jobs.

On Monday 21 October 2019, at 3.40 in the afternoon, fraud rule forty-one stopped a payment of forty thousand pounds from a savings account opened in 1989 to an account at a crypto exchange, and the case dropped into his queue with a small orange flag. Deb, from the next bank of desks, leaned round her monitor.

"Ooh. Forty grand to bitcoin. That's a romance scam, that is. Some bloke in Ghana with a picture of a helicopter."

"Could be," Kieran said. "Probably not. Sorry, Deb, I just need to ring her."

He rang her.

"Hello, is that Mrs Ashby? Sorry to bother you. My name's Kieran, I'm calling from the White Hart's payments team about a transfer you made earlier today. It's nothing to worry about, it's just a check we do."

"Barbara," said the voice. "Never Mrs. You've got a script, haven't you. Go on, then. Read it to me."

He read her the script. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money. Have you been promised a return. Has anyone asked you to keep this conversation private. Have you been asked to download anything onto your computer.

She answered each question exactly, like a woman filling in a form she had designed herself. No. No. No. No.

"Brilliant, thank you, that's really helpful," he said, which was what you said. "I'll just make a note and then I can—"

"My turn," she said. "How old is the system you're sat in?"

"Sorry?"

"The clearing. The thing that runs every night and takes three days to move money that could move in a second. How old is it?"

He found, with a small cold shock, that he did not know. "It's been updated a lot," he said. "I think."

"I updated it," said Barbara Ashby. "From 1988 to 2013, at a bureau in Reading. I wrote the bit that waits. It was built for magnetic tape, my love. It's got more patches on it than my dad's allotment shed. Do you know who could switch it off?"

"I'm not sure that's really—"

"No," she said. "You don't. Nobody on your floor does. That's not your fault. What's your surname, Kieran?"

He told her, because nothing in the script told him not to. He released the payment at 4.55, because nothing in the script allowed him to stop it, and he went home on the bus with the feeling of having walked past a door he had not known was in the wall. He had been past it every day for nine years.

## Two: Rabbit

The envelope came up from the post room on Friday 1 November, addressed in blue biro capitals: MR K. HALE, PAYMENTS EXCEPTIONS, THIRD FLOOR, WHITE HART HOUSE. It was the only letter anyone on the floor had received by name in a year, and Deb made a noise like a kettle coming to the boil.

"Is it a love letter? Kieran. Is it from the helicopter man?"

"It's a customer thing," he said. "It's fine. Honestly. It's just a thank-you, I think."

Inside was a white index card and a sheet of lined paper torn from a pad.

The card had twelve words on it in the same capitals, numbered, in two columns. They were ordinary words, some of them almost a poem, and he read them twice and then again, and they did not become a sentence. The note said: There is fifty pounds behind these. It is yours. Go and look. B.A.

He knew what the words were before he let himself know it. He had cleared enough payments to exchanges and read enough of the fraud team's bulletins. It was a recovery phrase: twelve words from a fixed list which were, in the new world, the whole of a wallet. Whoever held the words held the money. There was no branch behind them, no mural, no brass hart, no three-day cycle, no exceptions queue. Just the words.

Gifts from customers had to be declared. It was in the handbook, section nine, and in the online course he did every March, the one with the cartoon of a man holding a box of chocolates with a red cross over it. Declaring it would mean a form, and the form would mean Compliance, and Compliance would mean a note on Barbara Ashby's file. It would make her a case. It would make him a colleague who had accepted something.

In the guild there had been a rule, never written down, that you did not take loot you had not earned in front of people who had. He thought about that. Then he thought that there was nobody in front of him.

He put the card in his wallet, behind his bus pass, and did not declare it. He did not tell Laura either. It was the first thing in five years he had not told Laura, and it sat behind the bus pass like a second, smaller heart.

He did not type the words in. He looked them up once, on his phone in the third-floor toilets, confirmed what they were, and put the phone away as if it had bitten him. For four months the card rode in his wallet across the town and back. It softened at the corners. Every scam call he made after that had a second voice in it.

"Has anyone asked you to move your money?" he would say, and in his head Barbara said: How old is the thing you're sat in?

"Have you been promised a return?" And: Who could switch it off?

The routine did not change at all. It only began, faintly, to feel like a costume, the way the Hart's fleece did on dress-down Fridays: warm, well-made, and somebody else's.

In the canteen the founders looked down from their mural with their moustaches and their surprise. He found himself wondering what the back room of the White Hart had smelt of in 1868, beer and coal smoke and wet wool, and whether any of those men had lain awake afraid that the thing they were building would not hold.

At the Thursday quiz the question was which animal Alice followed down the hole, and everyone at the table wrote rabbit, and they came third.

## Three: Mirror

On the evening of Thursday 12 March 2020 the news said the markets had had their worst day since 1987 and bitcoin had lost half its value in a day. Laura was on a long and would not be home until after nine. The flat smelt of the pasta bake she had left him, covered in foil, with a note that said 40 MINS 180 LOVE YOU. Kieran stood at the kitchen table with the television on in the other room and took the card out of his wallet.

He downloaded a wallet app. It asked him whether he wanted to create a new wallet or restore one. It felt like the character-select screen of a game he had not played for ten years: new, or continue. He pressed restore. It gave him twelve empty boxes, and he typed the words into them one by one, the way you enter a code on a door you are not sure you are allowed to open.

Confirm, said the screen.

He put his thumb on the glass. For a moment nothing happened, and the black surface of the phone gave him back his own face, lit from below, a man in a kitchen in Old Swindon in a Hart fleece, tired, ordinary. Then the glass seemed to soften under his thumb, the way a puddle gives when you step in it expecting ground, and the balance rose out of nothing.

0.0071 BTC. £28.14.

Half of what she had given him. It had been fifty pounds when she sent it. The first thing the other world taught him was that it moved while you watched it.

"Right," he said aloud, to nobody. "Okay. Right."

Then he found the explorer, the public ledger, and pasted in his address, and there was the transaction that had sent him the coins on 31 October 2019, and the one before that which had sent them to her, and behind that another, and another: a chain running back through thousands of hands to January 2009. There was no queue. There was no cut-off. There were no exceptions, only confirmations. Anyone could read it. Anyone in the world. He sat down on the kitchen chair so suddenly that it scraped.

It was like the moment in the old game when you rode out of the starting valley for the first time and the map, which you had thought was the world, turned out to be a green corner of a continent, with the rest of it black at the edges, waiting. New area discovered. He had forgotten that feeling. It had not forgotten him.

Laura came in at twenty past nine and found him still at the table with the pasta bake cold in its foil.

"You haven't eaten," she said. "Kieran. It said forty minutes. It was on the note."

"Sorry. Sorry. I got caught up. The markets are mad."

"Everything's mad." She put her bag down very carefully, the way she did after a bad shift, as if the floor might go. "They're saying it's going to be us next. Like Italy. We're rehearsing proning tomorrow." She saw his face and stopped. "It's fine. Eat your tea."

On 23 March the country locked down. Laura went onto the COVID intensive-care unit at the hospital on the edge of town and began sleeping in the day with a towel over the curtain rail. Kieran carried the batch home on a laptop and set it up in the spare room, and the Hart shrank to a screen, and the week lost its shape. There was no bus, no tea round, no quiz. There was the laptop, and the phone, and the card, and every night after the file went, the black edges of the map.

He did not trade. He read. He read until three in the morning, the way he had read patch notes at fifteen, and in the day he cleared the exceptions queue from the spare room with Laura asleep through the wall. When she woke they talked about the car park and the canteen and whether the Co-op had flour, because there was nothing either of them could say about their real day that would not frighten the other.

## Four: Field

What happened that summer happened to everyone who was there, and they all said the same thing about it afterwards, and the thing they said was: what the actual hell was that.

In the middle of June a lending protocol started paying its users in a new token just for using it, and within a fortnight there were pools, and pools of pools, and tokens named after food, and people moving money between them at three in the morning to chase yields of four hundred, nine hundred, two thousand percent a year. Kieran read the words and none of them meant anything. Liquidity. Impermanent loss. Governance. Gas. Staking. Slippage. Rug. APY, APR, TVL, LP, DAO, ape, degen, wen, ser, gm. A new word every hour, a new protocol every day, and every protocol named after something you could eat. A sushi one. A pasta one. A pickle one. A yam, which lasted about a day and a half and then broke, and everybody laughed about it, and some people lost their houses.

"OMG," he typed into a search bar at two in the morning, and then, because it was two in the morning, "WTF is impermanent loss."

He learned it anyway. He learned it the way he had learned the game: by doing the stupid thing first and reading the forums after. A link in a thread about a token named after a vegetable took him to a chat server called the Sharehouse, where a few thousand anonymous people with cartoon avatars were awake in every time zone at once.

gm, said the channel, at 3.14 a.m. British Summer Time.

He asked a question about why his swap had failed, and a man in Ohio with a frog for a face answered it in forty seconds, and a woman in Manila with a cat in sunglasses corrected the man in Ohio, and they argued, and in the end somebody called Dmitri with no avatar at all posted a diagram. Kieran understood the diagram. He understood it the way he had understood a raid boss's patterns at fifteen: once, completely, and then in his sleep. The next week somebody new asked the same question, and he answered it before anyone else could.

ser you are a legend, typed the frog. who is this guy

kez, typed Dmitri. kez knows.

He sat in the blue light of the spare room with his heart going like a boy's.

In July he put in six thousand pounds: his half of a holiday fund the pandemic had cancelled, Crete, Laura's first holiday abroad since a school trip to Calais. It was his money. It was not the deposit. He told himself that on the landing, with his hand on the bathroom door, and it was true.

He began to farm. That was the word. You put your seed into a field and it grew and you harvested it and replanted, and the fields were open all night, and the crop did not care what time it was in Wiltshire. He set alarms for three and five, and he knew which pools would thin at which hours the way his ancestors must have known the weather coming in over the downs. It did not occur to him until much later that he was not farming the tokens so much as they were farming him: that what he had planted was his own sleep, and that it was being harvested every night at three.

The yields were real, some of them. By Christmas his six thousand was fifteen. By February it was thirty-eight.

"You're up early," Laura said once, coming in from a night at seven and finding him at the laptop in his dressing gown.

"Couldn't sleep," he said, and closed the lid, not quickly, the way you close a book you are allowed to read.

In September, when people were allowed to meet outdoors in sixes, Barbara emailed him from the address on her letter. He had answered it in March with a single line, thank you, I looked. She wrote back: Good. Do you walk?

## Five: Tribe

They met in the car park above Avebury on a Sunday, with the wind coming across the downs like something with a job to do. She was smaller than her voice, grey-haired, in a waxed jacket older than he was, with a collie that ignored him completely. She did not shake hands. She looked him up and down the way you look at a fuse board.

"You're taller than you sound," she said. "Come on. She needs walking, and so do you, by the look of you."

She walked faster than he did. They went up onto the Ridgeway, the chalk track white between the fields, and she told him about herself in the order she chose, in short sentences, like someone reading off a wiring diagram.

"Rodbourne. Dad was a boilermaker in the works, Grandad before him. I wanted to be an electrician from when I was ten. Mended the wireless with a hairgrip. Nobody took it serious." A stile; she went over it without using her hands. "1969 they let me in as an apprentice. Three women in the whole works. The men called me Babs. I didn't answer to it. After about a year they stopped."

"That must have been—"

"It was a job. I was good at it. Seventeen years." She walked on. "Then March '86, and they shut the lot. Hundred and forty years, and the hooter just stops. Whole town's clock, that hooter. People who never set foot in the works set their kitchen clocks by it. And then it doesn't go, and you're thirty-three with a trade nobody wants."

"What did you do?"

"Learned computers. Nights at the tech. First in the exam." She did not say it proudly; she said it the way you read a meter. "The bureau took me on in '88. Twenty-five years keeping the clearing standing up. Nobody ever put my name on anything. That's fine. I know what I built. I know where the bodges are, too."

"And now you don't trust it."

"I watched it nearly stop in 2008." For the first time she slowed. "Money going out one end, not coming in the other, the whole thing holding its breath for a weekend. My code, waiting. I didn't know if it'd arrive. Then someone wrote nine pages about a ledger nobody can switch off, and I read it three times on the train to Reading. Bought my first coins the year I retired. Never sold one." She turned her head. "What are you in? Not coins. Don't tell me you're in the casino."

He told her about the Sharehouse, the pools, the farms, the thirty-eight thousand. She laughed: one short bark, not unkind, not kind either.

"Right," she said. "So you've swapped a bank you can't see for a hundred blokes with cartoon faces you can't see. At least the bank's got a mural."

"It's not like that. They're really good, some of them. There's this guy Dmitri, he—"

"Mind," she said, and stopped, and pointed.

Below them the great stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and leaning, some of them taller than a double-decker, with sheep grazing between them and a pub in the middle.

"Farmers put those there," she said. "Four and a half thousand years ago. Dragged them across the downs on sledges. Forty tons, some of them. Took generations. Do you know why?"

"Religion?"

"That's what the signs say. I'll tell you what I think, for nothing." She pulled the collie's ear. "They wanted somewhere to gather. People will do almost anything to have a place to gather. That chat room of yours is the same animal. Worse food."

He did not tell her that she was wrong, because she was not, exactly. He did not tell her either that the Sharehouse was the first band since the guild that needed him, or that when he typed and forty people answered he felt what the men in the back room of the White Hart must have felt: that he was one of them, that they were building something, that it would hold.

At the car park she opened her boot and the collie jumped in.

"Same time next month," she said. It was not a question. "Wear proper shoes."

They walked most months after that. She never once asked him to stop. She had never been anybody's mother and did not mean to start.

## Six: Harvest

In January 2021 the third lockdown sent Laura back onto the unit for the worst weeks of the whole thing. She came home in the dark and sat in the car outside the flat for twenty minutes before she came up. Kieran watched her from the spare-room window, the dome light off, her hands on the wheel, and did not go down, because he thought she wanted to be alone, and because he did not know what he would say if he did.

When she came up she took her uniform off in the hallway and put it straight into the machine on a sixty, and stood in the shower until the hot water ran out.

"How was it?" he said, when she came out.

"We lost two." She was towelling her hair, not looking at him. "One was forty-one. Sats wouldn't come up. We proned him four times." She hung the towel on the radiator, square, the way she hung everything. "It's fine. What did you do?"

"Nothing. Work. The batch." He had made nine thousand pounds that week. He did not say it. It would have sounded like a different language, and it was.

Later she lay on the sofa scrolling through houses in Wroughton on her phone. It was the only thing she looked at that was not a monitor. A three-bed semi with a front door painted green. A garden with a shed.

"That one's got a porch," she said. "Look. You could put your boots in it."

"It's nice."

"It's got a front door that's ours." She said it lightly, the way she said important things, so that they could be taken as a joke if they had to be. She had been moved on from five rented houses before she was thirteen. In eight years with him she had never once asked for anything except that.

The market went up. By February his six thousand was thirty-eight thousand pounds, then forty-five. The Sharehouse had grown to four thousand members. People asked for Kez by name.

kez is this safe

kez wen new pool

kez my mum wants to get in, what do i tell her

tell her only what she can lose, he wrote, and felt wise, and did not think about his own mother.

In June the Hart's board decided to block payments to several crypto exchanges, after the regulator's warnings and a run of scam losses, and because Kieran knew better than anyone on the floor why a payment failed, they seconded him to the project that built the block. He sat in meetings about it on Teams with his camera on and his lanyard round his neck, saying things like "I think if we just look at the merchant category codes we'll catch most of it," and his project manager said, "Brilliant, Kieran, that's exactly the kind of thing." At night he walked round it through a payments app the Hart had never heard of. He did not feel he was lying. He felt, if anything, that he was the only person in either room who could see both rooms.

At the end of July the car plant made its last car. His father worked the final shift and came home with his hi-vis vest and a commemorative mug and put the mug in the cupboard over the kettle and did not take it out again.

"They gave us a mug," he said at Sunday lunch.

"Dad. I'm so sorry."

"It's only a mug." He carved. "Pass the gravy."

For three months Kieran paid his parents' rent, from his gains, by bank transfer on the first of the month, with the reference MUM AND DAD. His father thanked him once, in the car outside the Job Centre, without looking at him.

"You didn't have to."

"I wanted to."

"Well." His father looked at the Job Centre's glass door, and at his own reflection in it. "Your grandad would have liked that. Doesn't matter where it comes from, he used to say, as long as it comes."

It was the best thing the money ever did. It was also the thing that made him certain the money was real: that the game was not a game, that he had been right, that everybody who had laughed at the food tokens was wrong.

By November 2021 his screen said he was worth seventy-one thousand pounds. Laura had a shortlist of three houses and a folder called HOUSE on the laptop they shared, which he never opened. His alarms went at three and five. The rota on the fridge said LONG, LONG, OFF, NIGHT, NIGHT, NIGHT, and he read it every morning the way he read the price: to know what was coming.

## Seven: Anchor

After November the prices went down, the way weather goes down, gradually and then all at once and then gradually again. By Christmas his seventy-one thousand was fifty. By the new year it was forty-five. The Sharehouse stopped talking about moons.

zoom out

it's a healthy correction ser

anyone who sells here deserves to be poor

smart money is going to safe yield, typed Dmitri. twenty percent on a dollar. it literally does not move.

Safe yield was a dollar. Not a real dollar, but a token that was always worth a dollar, and a savings protocol that paid nearly twenty percent a year for holding it. The token did not keep its dollar with dollars in a vault. It kept it with a trick of arithmetic: anyone could always swap one of them for a dollar's worth of a sister token, and back, and all that swapping held the price in place like a tent held by its guy ropes. Kieran read the paper. He understood it. He understood that the ropes held only while the sister token was worth more than all the dollars tied to it, and he understood that everybody in the tent believed it would hold, and that the believing was part of what held it. It was a peg. Everything he had ever worked in was a peg, when you looked: the Hart's deposits, the three-day cycle, the plant, the works. They held because everyone agreed they would.

House prices in Wroughton had gone up faster than the deposit. The deposit was twenty-four thousand pounds in a savings account at the Hart, earning nothing, and fifteen thousand of it was Laura's overtime from two pandemic winters.

On a Sunday in January he said it at the kitchen table. The rota was on the fridge behind her. She had the calendar open and was writing viewings into it in green pen.

"Lor. Can I— sorry, can I just run something past you? It's a money thing. It's boring."

"Mm."

"I want to move the deposit somewhere it'll actually earn something before we buy. Twenty percent. It's pegged to the dollar. It doesn't go up and down like the other stuff. It's basically a savings account, just— better."

She did not look up for long. She had worked seven nights out of the last ten, and the green pen was running out.

"You're the one who knows about money," she said. "Do what you think."

"Are you sure? I can explain how it—"

"Kieran." She shook the pen. "I trust you. Do what you think."

He did not explain what a stablecoin was, and she did not ask. By the end of the week the twenty-four thousand pounds had left the Hart through the app it had never heard of and become twenty-four thousand pounds' worth of dollars that did not move, earning twenty percent. He checked it every morning like a man checking a sleeping child.

In February he told Barbara on the Ridgeway, walking towards the long barrow, pleased with himself.

She stopped walking. She looked at the barrow for a long time, at the great sarsens of its doorway, and he waited for her to say the thing about casinos.

"That's yours and Laura's to decide," she said.

"But what do you think? Honestly. You know how these things work better than—"

"I think it's yours and Laura's to decide." She clicked her tongue at the collie. "Come on. Dog's getting cold."

She said nothing else about it all afternoon. She talked about the men who had dragged the stones, and about the collie's hips, and about a programmer she had worked with in 1994 who had kept a ferret in his desk drawer. On the drive home she thought of three sentences she could have said, one of them about guy ropes, and said none of them out loud, even to the dog, because she had never been anybody's mother and did not mean to start at sixty-eight.

## Eight: Hole

On Saturday 7 May the dollar that did not move moved. Ninety-nine cents, then back. The Sharehouse made jokes about it.

lol the dollar is at 0.99 i'm literally shaking

buy the dip on a dollar

On Monday evening, the ninth, it was ninety-eight, then ninety-three, then eighty. Laura was on nights; the rota said so. Kieran sat at the spare-room desk with the Sharehouse open on one screen and the price on the other, and he did not sleep, and the ropes went one by one.

He knew exactly what was happening. That was the worst of it. People were selling the dollar and swapping it for the sister token, and each swap made new sister tokens out of nothing, and each new sister token made the others worth less, so each swap needed more of them, and the fall fed itself like a fire in a draught. He could have drawn it on a whiteboard. He could have written the Hart's fraud team a short, excellent email about it, with a diagram.

it has always come back, said the Sharehouse.

The founder's account posted that he was deploying more capital. A thousand cartoon faces reposted it. It was like the old raids, when the tank died and the healer died and forty people on voice chat were shouting, and the one thing you never did, the thing people never forgave, was leave. Hold. Hold. Everybody hold.

On Tuesday it touched sixty cents and bounced to ninety and the channel went up like a crowd at a match.

He could have sold at ninety. He could have sold at sixty. He did not, because selling would make it real, and because leaving the band while it was losing was the one thing he had never forgiven himself for, since the night at twenty when he had handed the guild to Sander and logged off in the middle of a raid. The oldest part of him, the part older than the Hart and older than the stones, knew that the worst thing that could happen to a person was to be alone outside the fire. It did not know about percentages. It had never heard of a peg.

By Thursday it was twenty cents. By Friday the thirteenth the deposit was worth about three thousand pounds and falling. The sister token was worth almost nothing. The Sharehouse had gone from certainty to instructions to silence to jokes to goodbyes.

That same week the Hart's fraud lines filled with customers who had lost their savings, some of them through the same apps Kieran had used to walk round the block he had built, and the payments team was pulled onto the phones.

On Wednesday he took a call from a man in Stratton who had put in his redundancy money.

"I'm sixty-one," the man said. "I was at the plant. Thirty years. They said it was a dollar. A dollar. Can you get it back? You're a bank. Can you get it back?"

"I'm really sorry, Mr Pritchard," Kieran said. "I'm just going to take a few details, and then I'll explain what we can and can't do. Is that okay?"

His own voice came out of his mouth steady and kind and professional, the voice he had used on Barbara, and the man began to cry, a sound like something tearing slowly, and Kieran said he was very sorry, and he was. After the call he sat with his headset on and the line dead and looked at the orange flags in the queue until Deb put a hand on his shoulder and asked if he wanted a tea.

"No, you're alright. Thanks, Deb. I'm fine."

He went home and checked a balance that was a number and then a smaller number.

## Nine: Silence

For five weeks he said nothing.

He went to work and came home. He took the calls. He checked the dead balance at night; by June it was a few hundred pounds, then less. He lost weight. His right hand shook when he held his phone, so he started holding it in his left. He slept three hours a night and told Laura it was the heat.

"You look grey," she said one morning, coming in off a night. "Are you eating?"

"I'm fine. Honestly. It's just work. The scam lines are mental at the moment."

"Get your bloods done." She was already in the kitchen, already opening the fridge. "I mean it. You look like my Tuesday admissions."

When the mortgage adviser emailed in the middle of June for their savings statements, Laura forwarded it to him with a thumbs-up and a small house.

He told her that evening at the kitchen table, with the rota on the fridge behind her. He had rehearsed it on the landing, on the bus, in the third-floor toilets, and none of the versions survived the first sentence.

"Lor. I need to tell you something and I need you to let me get to the end. Sorry. I'm sorry. I just need to get to the end."

He told her everything, in the order it had happened: January, the dollar that did not move, the ropes, the Tuesday bounce, the Friday, the five weeks. He told her how much, to the pound. His voice did the thing it did on the scam line, steady and professional, and he hated it, and he could not stop it, because it was the only voice he had that did not shake. He did not cry, because he did not think he was allowed to.

She listened. She did not interrupt once. When he stopped she asked three questions, the way she would have taken a history.

"How much is left?"

"About three hundred and forty pounds."

"Is any of it recoverable?"

"No. I don't think so. No."

"Does anyone else know?"

"Barbara. Some of it. Not all."

"Right," Laura said. "Okay."

Then she went to bed.

In the morning she moved their accounts into her own name, picked up two extra shifts a week, and cancelled the mortgage appointment with a polite email he found in the shared inbox: Due to a change in our circumstances. She did not shout. She did not cry in front of him. She had learned on five doorsteps, watching her mother hand back keys to men who did not look at her, that you hold everything together and you say nothing, and she was very good at it, and she held.

The flat went quiet in a new way. Before, they had been two people with screens: hers the monitors, his the charts. Now there was one screen fewer and no more words. The rota stayed on the fridge. He read it every morning to know when she would be asleep, awake or gone, and understood for the first time that for two years he had been reading it as a timetable of when nobody would be watching.

In July he walked with Barbara and told her the rest. She did not laugh, and she did not say anything about casinos. She walked a long way without speaking, faster than him, the collie trotting to keep up, and at the long barrow she stopped and put her hand flat on one of the great stones, as if she were checking it for current.

"I gave you a key," she said, "and not a map."

"It's not your fault."

"I didn't say it was my fault. I said what I did." She took her hand off the stone. "Those are different things. You'll learn that."

Then she said nothing else, all the way down, and he thought that was the end of it.

## Ten: Stone

On a weekday in August, while Kieran was at the Hart, Barbara Ashby took the bus into Swindon, which she had not done in two years, and walked up Wood Street, and knocked on the door of a flat above a shop.

Laura answered in a hoodie and pyjama bottoms, between nights, with her hair tied up. She did not know who this small grey woman in a waxed jacket was.

"You're Laura," the woman said. "I'm Barbara. I'm the one who sent him the card. Can I come in? I won't stay. I don't sit down in other people's kitchens, so you needn't bother with the kettle."

She did not sit down. She stood in the kitchen by the fridge with the rota on it and told Laura what she had done, in short flat sentences, like someone reading out a fault report.

"I gave him a key without a map. I checked the key and not the person. That's the opposite of everything I believe, and I did it anyway, because I liked his voice on the phone and I thought, there's one who could see it. In February he told me where your deposit was. I knew how that thing could fail. I could have drawn it for him on the back of a car-park ticket. I said it was his choice and nothing else, because I have never been anybody's mother, and I have been very proud of that for sixty-nine years." She looked at the rota. "That part's mine. The rest is his. I'm not here to make it better. I can't. I'm here because somebody in this should say it out loud to you, and it isn't going to be him, and by the look of you it isn't going to be you either."

Laura stood with her arms folded. "You don't know anything about me."

"No. That's fair." Barbara looked at her properly then, for the first time. "I know what holding everything looks like. I held a whole works together for seventeen years, one fuse at a time, and nobody ever asked me how. You're allowed to say something. That's all. That's all I came to tell you. You're allowed."

When she had gone, Laura sat at the kitchen table for a long time. Then she took the rota off the fridge, and looked at the months of it, the longs and the nights and the days, and put it back, square, under the magnet from Weymouth.

That evening, when Kieran came in, she was sitting at the table with a notepad, the way she sat at handover.

"Sit down," she said. "I'm going to do this how I know how to do it, or I won't be able to do it at all. Don't interrupt."

He sat.

"Situation," Laura said. "Our deposit's gone. Fifteen thousand of it was mine. It was every night shift I did in the pandemic. Every one." She did not look at the notepad. "Background. I've been moved out of five houses. The last one I was twelve and I carried my stuff to the car in a bin bag because we'd run out of boxes, and I decided, I'm never doing that again. Never. That money was a front door. That's what it was. It wasn't money." She breathed in, out, the way she taught relatives to. "Assessment. You lied to me for five weeks. You went to work and came home and ate your tea and lied. That's worse than the money. It's so much worse than the money, Kieran. And I said do what you think. I know I did. I said it because I was so tired of being the one who holds everything that I handed it to you without looking. So that's mine. Barbara's right. That bit's mine." Her voice went, and came back. "Recommendation."

She stopped. It was the only line she had not written down.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't know. I want you to tell me what we do."

He did not answer with the scam-line voice. He did not have it any more. He reached across the table and put his hand next to hers, not on it, where she could take it or not.

"We tell each other everything," he said. "Every number. Every time. Even the boring ones. And we start again, slowly. And I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Lor."

She did not take his hand. She did not move hers away. It was the first conversation they had had in two years that neither of them was having through a screen, and it went on until after one in the morning, and at the end of it she was the one who said, "Come to bed," and he was the one who cried.

## Eleven: Field, Again

They stayed. They chose it, which is different from staying.

They opened one account that both of them could see. They stuck a spreadsheet on the fridge next to the rota, held by a second magnet, from Lyme Regis. They postponed the house by two years. Kieran deleted the apps and told Laura every number, every time, until telling her every number stopped feeling like confession and started feeling like weather: something that came over the downs whether you minded or not, and that you talked about in the kitchen.

"Four hundred and twelve in the joint," he would say, coming in. "Car insurance is up."

"Course it is," she would say. "Everything's up. Put the kettle on."

Laura, who had never asked for anything, asked for Sundays off together, and got them. She was astonished at how easy it was. "You just ask," she said to her friend Jade on the unit. "You just say it. Who knew."

Rebuilding was slow and dull, and they chose that too. It was harvest in the old sense: the same field, the same work, every season, nothing that doubled overnight.

In November a big exchange collapsed in the background, and it turned out its customers' money had been used by its trading firm, and bitcoin fell to a quarter of its peak. Barbara's coins fell with it.

"You're very calm about it," Kieran said on the Ridgeway.

"I've seen three of these. Four, if you count 2008, and I do." She walked on. "The coins aren't the point. The point's that nobody can switch them off. Price is just weather."

She did not sell. And on the walks she began, unasked, to explain things to him. How the clearing actually worked, file by file, down to the byte. Why the bit that waits had been written to wait: because in 1988 you could not be sure the other bank existed until morning. What a key was, and what a map was, and the difference.

"A key gets you in," she said. "A map tells you where the floor stops. I gave you the one without the other. So." She tapped her head. "Map. Month by month. You'll hate it. Wear proper shoes."

The walks became lessons, and he let them.

Half the Sharehouse's channels were empty now. A few regulars still posted gm in the mornings, from Ohio and Lisbon and Manila. Dmitri had gone; his account said deleted user. Kieran looked in sometimes. He did not trade. Once, at three in the morning, a newcomer asked whether it was too late to get in, and Kieran wrote a long careful message about pegs and guy ropes and fires in draughts, and then deleted it, and wrote instead: ask someone who loves you to sit with you while you decide.

The newcomer replied: lol ok boomer.

He closed the laptop and went to bed, where Laura was asleep, on a day off, next to him.

## Twelve: Awake

He stayed on the payments-exceptions desk at the Hart. The routine was the same. He was not.

He knew how old the machine was now, and what ran under it, and who could switch it off. He knew what the new machine had done to him in the night. He kept the card in his wallet behind his bus pass, soft as cloth now, with its 0.0071 bitcoin still behind it, worth about a hundred and fifty pounds that spring. He had never spent it and did not mean to. It was not a key any more. It was a reminder, kept by choice, the way his grandfather Ron had kept a brass works tally token in his pocket for fifteen years after the hooter stopped.

In March 2023 fraud rule forty-one stopped a payment of twelve thousand pounds from a saver in her twenties to a crypto exchange, and the case dropped into his queue with its small orange flag, and he rang her.

"Hi, is that Chloe? Sorry to bother you. My name's Kieran, I'm calling from the White Hart's payments team about a transfer you made this morning. It's just a check we do."

He read her the script. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money. Have you been promised a return. Has anyone asked you to keep this conversation private.

She said no to all of it, a little impatiently, the way he would have.

"Brilliant. Thank you. That's everything I need." He looked at the flag. "Can I ask you something that isn't on the script? You can tell me to get lost."

"Go on."

"Do you know how old the thing is that you're leaving? And how new the thing is that you're going into?" He waited. "And if it goes wrong, who are you going to tell?"

There was a silence on the line. It was not a hostile silence. It was the silence of somebody walking past a door they had not known was in the wall.

"My brother, probably," she said at last. "He'd kill me."

"Tell him before," Kieran said. "Not after. That's all. Good luck, Chloe."

He released the payment at 4.55, because nothing allowed him to stop it, and because it was hers, and he hoped she would tell her brother before.

On the Sunday the three of them walked on the Ridgeway: Barbara in front with the collie, too fast; Laura, who had never been up there, in the middle, in new boots; Kieran behind. The chalk track ran white between the fields, as it had for five thousand years. Below them the stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and leaning, with sheep between them and smoke from the pub chimney.

"Why would anyone drag them all that way?" Laura asked.

"To gather," Kieran said. "People will do almost anything to have somewhere to gather."

Barbara snorted, because it was her line. "He steals everything," she said to Laura. "Watch your purse."

"I know," Laura said. "He's got my deposit."

It was the first time she had made a joke about it. Barbara laughed, the short bark, and Kieran laughed too, a beat late, the way you laugh when you have been let back in.

His phone was in the car. The card was in his wallet. Three clocks ran in him still, and would all his life: the body's, which wanted the fire and the faces; the institution's, which closed at five and waited three days; and the machine's, which never closed at all. He could hear all three now. That was the difference. For most of his life he had heard only one of them, and thought it was the world.

The wind came over the downs. Laura took his hand without looking, the way you do when you are sure it will be there. They walked on towards the long barrow, three small people on the oldest road in Britain, in the corner of the map they had chosen, awake.
