## Nine: Silence

For five weeks he said nothing.

He went to work and came home. He took the calls. He checked the dead balance at night; by June it was a few hundred pounds, then less. He lost weight. His right hand shook when he held his phone, so he started holding it in his left. He slept three hours a night and told Laura it was the heat.

"You look grey," she said one morning, coming in off a night. "Are you eating?"

"I'm fine. Honestly. It's just work. The scam lines are mental at the moment."

"Get your bloods done." She was already in the kitchen, already opening the fridge. "I mean it. You look like my Tuesday admissions."

When the mortgage adviser emailed in the middle of June for their savings statements, Laura forwarded it to him with a thumbs-up and a small house.

He told her that evening at the kitchen table, with the rota on the fridge behind her. He had rehearsed it on the landing, on the bus, in the third-floor toilets, and none of the versions survived the first sentence.

"Lor. I need to tell you something and I need you to let me get to the end. Sorry. I'm sorry. I just need to get to the end."

He told her everything, in the order it had happened: January, the dollar that did not move, the ropes, the Tuesday bounce, the Friday, the five weeks. He told her how much, to the pound. His voice did the thing it did on the scam line, steady and professional, and he hated it, and he could not stop it, because it was the only voice he had that did not shake. He did not cry, because he did not think he was allowed to.

She listened. She did not interrupt once. When he stopped she asked three questions, the way she would have taken a history.

"How much is left?"

"About three hundred and forty pounds."

"Is any of it recoverable?"

"No. I don't think so. No."

"Does anyone else know?"

"Barbara. Some of it. Not all."

"Right," Laura said. "Okay."

Then she went to bed.

In the morning she moved their accounts into her own name, picked up two extra shifts a week, and cancelled the mortgage appointment with a polite email he found in the shared inbox: Due to a change in our circumstances. She did not shout. She did not cry in front of him. She had learned on five doorsteps, watching her mother hand back keys to men who did not look at her, that you hold everything together and you say nothing, and she was very good at it, and she held.

The flat went quiet in a new way. Before, they had been two people with screens: hers the monitors, his the charts. Now there was one screen fewer and no more words. The rota stayed on the fridge. He read it every morning to know when she would be asleep, awake or gone, and understood for the first time that for two years he had been reading it as a timetable of when nobody would be watching.

In July he walked with Barbara and told her the rest. She did not laugh, and she did not say anything about casinos. She walked a long way without speaking, faster than him, the collie trotting to keep up, and at the long barrow she stopped and put her hand flat on one of the great stones, as if she were checking it for current.

"I gave you a key," she said, "and not a map."

"It's not your fault."

"I didn't say it was my fault. I said what I did." She took her hand off the stone. "Those are different things. You'll learn that."

Then she said nothing else, all the way down, and he thought that was the end of it.

## Ten: Stone

On a weekday in August, while Kieran was at the Hart, Barbara Ashby took the bus into Swindon, which she had not done in two years, and walked up Wood Street, and knocked on the door of a flat above a shop.

Laura answered in a hoodie and pyjama bottoms, between nights, with her hair tied up. She did not know who this small grey woman in a waxed jacket was.

"You're Laura," the woman said. "I'm Barbara. I'm the one who sent him the card. Can I come in? I won't stay. I don't sit down in other people's kitchens, so you needn't bother with the kettle."

She did not sit down. She stood in the kitchen by the fridge with the rota on it and told Laura what she had done, in short flat sentences, like someone reading out a fault report.

"I gave him a key without a map. I checked the key and not the person. That's the opposite of everything I believe, and I did it anyway, because I liked his voice on the phone and I thought, there's one who could see it. In February he told me where your deposit was. I knew how that thing could fail. I could have drawn it for him on the back of a car-park ticket. I said it was his choice and nothing else, because I have never been anybody's mother, and I have been very proud of that for sixty-nine years." She looked at the rota. "That part's mine. The rest is his. I'm not here to make it better. I can't. I'm here because somebody in this should say it out loud to you, and it isn't going to be him, and by the look of you it isn't going to be you either."

Laura stood with her arms folded. "You don't know anything about me."

"No. That's fair." Barbara looked at her properly then, for the first time. "I know what holding everything looks like. I held a whole works together for seventeen years, one fuse at a time, and nobody ever asked me how. You're allowed to say something. That's all. That's all I came to tell you. You're allowed."

When she had gone, Laura sat at the kitchen table for a long time. Then she took the rota off the fridge, and looked at the months of it, the longs and the nights and the days, and put it back, square, under the magnet from Weymouth.

That evening, when Kieran came in, she was sitting at the table with a notepad, the way she sat at handover.

"Sit down," she said. "I'm going to do this how I know how to do it, or I won't be able to do it at all. Don't interrupt."

He sat.

"Situation," Laura said. "Our deposit's gone. Fifteen thousand of it was mine. It was every night shift I did in the pandemic. Every one." She did not look at the notepad. "Background. I've been moved out of five houses. The last one I was twelve and I carried my stuff to the car in a bin bag because we'd run out of boxes, and I decided, I'm never doing that again. Never. That money was a front door. That's what it was. It wasn't money." She breathed in, out, the way she taught relatives to. "Assessment. You lied to me for five weeks. You went to work and came home and ate your tea and lied. That's worse than the money. It's so much worse than the money, Kieran. And I said do what you think. I know I did. I said it because I was so tired of being the one who holds everything that I handed it to you without looking. So that's mine. Barbara's right. That bit's mine." Her voice went, and came back. "Recommendation."

She stopped. It was the only line she had not written down.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't know. I want you to tell me what we do."

He did not answer with the scam-line voice. He did not have it any more. He reached across the table and put his hand next to hers, not on it, where she could take it or not.

"We tell each other everything," he said. "Every number. Every time. Even the boring ones. And we start again, slowly. And I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Lor."

She did not take his hand. She did not move hers away. It was the first conversation they had had in two years that neither of them was having through a screen, and it went on until after one in the morning, and at the end of it she was the one who said, "Come to bed," and he was the one who cried.

## Eleven: Field, Again

They stayed. They chose it, which is different from staying.

They opened one account that both of them could see. They stuck a spreadsheet on the fridge next to the rota, held by a second magnet, from Lyme Regis. They postponed the house by two years. Kieran deleted the apps and told Laura every number, every time, until telling her every number stopped feeling like confession and started feeling like weather: something that came over the downs whether you minded or not, and that you talked about in the kitchen.

"Four hundred and twelve in the joint," he would say, coming in. "Car insurance is up."

"Course it is," she would say. "Everything's up. Put the kettle on."

Laura, who had never asked for anything, asked for Sundays off together, and got them. She was astonished at how easy it was. "You just ask," she said to her friend Jade on the unit. "You just say it. Who knew."

Rebuilding was slow and dull, and they chose that too. It was harvest in the old sense: the same field, the same work, every season, nothing that doubled overnight.

In November a big exchange collapsed in the background, and it turned out its customers' money had been used by its trading firm, and bitcoin fell to a quarter of its peak. Barbara's coins fell with it.

"You're very calm about it," Kieran said on the Ridgeway.

"I've seen three of these. Four, if you count 2008, and I do." She walked on. "The coins aren't the point. The point's that nobody can switch them off. Price is just weather."

She did not sell. And on the walks she began, unasked, to explain things to him. How the clearing actually worked, file by file, down to the byte. Why the bit that waits had been written to wait: because in 1988 you could not be sure the other bank existed until morning. What a key was, and what a map was, and the difference.

"A key gets you in," she said. "A map tells you where the floor stops. I gave you the one without the other. So." She tapped her head. "Map. Month by month. You'll hate it. Wear proper shoes."

The walks became lessons, and he let them.

Half the Sharehouse's channels were empty now. A few regulars still posted gm in the mornings, from Ohio and Lisbon and Manila. Dmitri had gone; his account said deleted user. Kieran looked in sometimes. He did not trade. Once, at three in the morning, a newcomer asked whether it was too late to get in, and Kieran wrote a long careful message about pegs and guy ropes and fires in draughts, and then deleted it, and wrote instead: ask someone who loves you to sit with you while you decide.

The newcomer replied: lol ok boomer.

He closed the laptop and went to bed, where Laura was asleep, on a day off, next to him.

## Twelve: Awake

He stayed on the payments-exceptions desk at the Hart. The routine was the same. He was not.

He knew how old the machine was now, and what ran under it, and who could switch it off. He knew what the new machine had done to him in the night. He kept the card in his wallet behind his bus pass, soft as cloth now, with its 0.0071 bitcoin still behind it, worth about a hundred and fifty pounds that spring. He had never spent it and did not mean to. It was not a key any more. It was a reminder, kept by choice, the way his grandfather Ron had kept a brass works tally token in his pocket for fifteen years after the hooter stopped.

In March 2023 fraud rule forty-one stopped a payment of twelve thousand pounds from a saver in her twenties to a crypto exchange, and the case dropped into his queue with its small orange flag, and he rang her.

"Hi, is that Chloe? Sorry to bother you. My name's Kieran, I'm calling from the White Hart's payments team about a transfer you made this morning. It's just a check we do."

He read her the script. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money. Have you been promised a return. Has anyone asked you to keep this conversation private.

She said no to all of it, a little impatiently, the way he would have.

"Brilliant. Thank you. That's everything I need." He looked at the flag. "Can I ask you something that isn't on the script? You can tell me to get lost."

"Go on."

"Do you know how old the thing is that you're leaving? And how new the thing is that you're going into?" He waited. "And if it goes wrong, who are you going to tell?"

There was a silence on the line. It was not a hostile silence. It was the silence of somebody walking past a door they had not known was in the wall.

"My brother, probably," she said at last. "He'd kill me."

"Tell him before," Kieran said. "Not after. That's all. Good luck, Chloe."

He released the payment at 4.55, because nothing allowed him to stop it, and because it was hers, and he hoped she would tell her brother before.

On the Sunday the three of them walked on the Ridgeway: Barbara in front with the collie, too fast; Laura, who had never been up there, in the middle, in new boots; Kieran behind. The chalk track ran white between the fields, as it had for five thousand years. Below them the stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and leaning, with sheep between them and smoke from the pub chimney.

"Why would anyone drag them all that way?" Laura asked.

"To gather," Kieran said. "People will do almost anything to have somewhere to gather."

Barbara snorted, because it was her line. "He steals everything," she said to Laura. "Watch your purse."

"I know," Laura said. "He's got my deposit."

It was the first time she had made a joke about it. Barbara laughed, the short bark, and Kieran laughed too, a beat late, the way you laugh when you have been let back in.

His phone was in the car. The card was in his wallet. Three clocks ran in him still, and would all his life: the body's, which wanted the fire and the faces; the institution's, which closed at five and waited three days; and the machine's, which never closed at all. He could hear all three now. That was the difference. For most of his life he had heard only one of them, and thought it was the world.

The wind came over the downs. Laura took his hand without looking, the way you do when you are sure it will be there. They walked on towards the long barrow, three small people on the oldest road in Britain, in the corner of the map they had chosen, awake.
