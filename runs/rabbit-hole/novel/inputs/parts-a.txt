## One: Blue

The payments file left White Hart House at ten every night and came back at seven every morning with its failures sorted into a queue, and Kieran Hale had cleared that queue for nine years. Closed account. Name mismatch. Sort code retired in 2011. Fraud rule forty-one. He could read an exception the way his grandfather Ron, a fitter in the railway works until they shut, had read a boiler by the sound of it.

The Hart had been founded in 1868 by railwaymen pooling their wages in the back room of a White Hart inn so that each of them, one by one, could buy a house. It still had the brass hart on the door and a mural of the founders in the canteen, men with moustaches and bowler hats looking faintly surprised to find themselves on a wall in west Swindon. Kieran liked the mural. He liked most things about the Hart. That was the problem, if it was a problem.

His week had one shape. The 8.12 bus from Old Town. The lanyard. The tea round, which went clockwise from the window and which nobody had ever agreed to and everybody kept. Cut-off at ten. Lunch at one. The Thursday quiz at the Beehive, where his team came third with the regularity of a tide. Laura's rota on the fridge, printed monthly, held up by a magnet from Weymouth: days, nights, the long days she called longs. Sunday lunch in Penhill, where his father talked about the car plant closing in 2021 as if it were weather, something that would come in over the downs whether you minded or not.

He was twenty-nine. He was reliable. His appraisals used the word. At sixteen he had led a raiding guild of forty people across six countries in an online game, set their timetables, known who was sulking in Lisbon and who needed carrying in Tallinn, and nobody on the third floor knew it. When he took the job at twenty he had logged off for the last time, given the guild to a Dutch boy called Sander, and become, deliberately, no trouble to anyone.

Some Sunday evenings the week sat on his chest like a cat. He did not mention it. It seemed an ungrateful thing to feel.

On Monday 21 October 2019, at 3.40 in the afternoon, fraud rule forty-one stopped a payment of forty thousand pounds from a savings account opened in 1989 to an account at a crypto exchange, and the system put the case in his queue, and he rang the customer.

"Mrs Ashby? My name's Kieran, I'm calling from the White Hart's payments team about a transfer you've made today."

"Barbara," said the voice. "Never Mrs. Go on, read me the script."

He read her the script. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money. Have you been promised a return. Has anyone asked you to keep this conversation private. Are you being asked to download anything onto your computer.

She answered each question exactly, like a woman filling in a form she had designed. No. No. No. No. Then she said, "My turn. How old is the system you're sitting in?"

"I'm sorry?"

"The clearing. The thing that runs every night and waits three days to move money that could move in a second. How old is it?"

He found that he did not know. "It's been updated," he said.

"I updated it," said Barbara Ashby. "From 1988 to 2013, at a bureau in Reading. I wrote the bit that waits. It was designed for magnetic tape, Kieran. Do you know who could switch it off?"

He did not know that either. He released the payment at 4.55, because nothing in the script allowed him to stop it, and went home on the bus with the feeling of having walked past a door he had not known was in the wall.

## Two: Rabbit

The envelope came up from the post room on Friday 1 November, addressed in blue capitals: MR K. HALE, PAYMENTS EXCEPTIONS, THIRD FLOOR, WHITE HART HOUSE. It was the only letter anyone on the floor had received by name in a year, and Deb from the next bank of desks made a noise like a kettle.

Inside was a white index card and a sheet of lined paper torn from a pad.

The card had twelve words on it in the same capitals, numbered, in two columns. They were ordinary words. Some of them were almost a poem. He read them twice and then again, and they did not become a sentence.

The note said: There is fifty pounds behind these. It is yours. Go and look. B.A.

He knew what the words were before he let himself know it. He had processed enough payments to exchanges and read enough of the fraud team's bulletins. It was a recovery phrase: twelve words from a fixed list that were, in the new world, the whole of a wallet. Whoever held the words held the money. There was no bank behind them, no branch, no mural, no brass hart. Just the words.

Gifts from customers had to be declared. It was in the handbook, section nine, and in the online course he did every March. Declaring it would mean a form, and the form would mean compliance, and compliance would mean a note on Barbara Ashby's file that said the kind of thing that notes on files said. It would make her a case. It would make him a colleague who had accepted something.

He put the card in his wallet, behind his bus pass, and did not declare it, and did not tell Laura, which was the first thing in five years he had not told Laura.

He did not type the words in. He looked them up once, on his phone in the toilets, and confirmed what they were, and put the phone away as if it had bitten him. For four months the card rode in his wallet across the town and back. It softened at the corners. Every scam call he made after that had a second voice in it, asking him how old the thing was that he was sitting in. The routine did not change at all. It began, faintly, to feel like a costume.

In the canteen the founders looked down from their mural with their moustaches and their surprise, and he found himself wondering what the back room of the White Hart had smelt of in 1868, and whether any of those men had lain awake afraid that the thing they were building would not hold.

## Three: Mirror

On the evening of Thursday 12 March 2020 the news said the markets had had their worst day since 1987 and bitcoin had lost half its value in a day. Laura was on a long and would not be home until after nine. Kieran stood at the kitchen table with the television on in the other room and took the card out of his wallet.

He downloaded a wallet app. It asked him whether he wanted to create a new wallet or restore one. He pressed restore. It gave him twelve empty boxes, and he typed the words into them one by one, the way you would enter a code on a door you were not sure you were allowed to open.

Confirm, said the screen.

He put his thumb on the glass. For a moment nothing happened, and the black surface of the phone gave him back his own face, lit from below, a man in a kitchen in Old Swindon in a Hart fleece. Then the glass seemed to soften under his thumb, the way a puddle gives when you step in it expecting ground, and the balance rose out of nothing.

0.0071 BTC. £28.14.

Half of what she had given him. The first thing the other world taught him was that it moved while you watched it.

Then he found the explorer, the public ledger, and pasted in his address, and there was the transaction that had sent him the coins on 31 October 2019, and the one before that which had sent them to her, and behind that another, and another, a chain running back through thousands of hands to January 2009. Anyone could read it. Anyone in the world. He sat down.

It was like the moment in the old game when you left the starting valley for the first time and the map, which you had thought was the world, turned out to be a green corner of a continent, with the rest of it black at the edges and waiting. He had forgotten that feeling. It had not forgotten him.

On 23 March the country locked down. Laura went onto the COVID intensive-care unit at the hospital on the edge of town and began sleeping in the day with a towel over the curtain rail. Kieran carried the batch home on a laptop and set it up in the spare room, and the Hart shrank to a screen, and the week lost its shape. There was no bus, no tea round, no quiz. There was the laptop, and the phone, and the card, and every night after the file went, the black edges of the map.

He did not trade. He read. He read until three in the morning, the way he had at fifteen, and in the day he cleared the exceptions queue from the spare room with Laura asleep through the wall, and neither of them said much, because there was nothing either of them could say about their day that would not frighten the other.

## Four: Field

What happened that summer happened to everyone who was there, and they all said the same thing about it, and the thing they said was: what the actual hell is this.

In the middle of June a lending protocol started paying its users in a new token for using it, and within a fortnight there were pools, and pools of pools, and tokens called after food, and people moving money between them at three in the morning to chase yields of four hundred, nine hundred, two thousand percent a year. Kieran read the words and none of them meant anything. Liquidity. Impermanent loss. Governance. Gas. Staking. Slippage. Rug. APY, APR, TVL, LP, DAO, ape, degen, wen, ser, gm. A new word every hour, and a new protocol every day, and every protocol named after something you could eat.

He learned it anyway. He learned it the way he had learned the game, by doing the stupid thing first and reading the forums after. A link in a thread about a token named after a vegetable took him to a chat server called the Sharehouse, where a few thousand anonymous people with cartoon avatars were awake in every time zone at once.

They answered his questions at three in the morning. They called him Kez, after the name on his wallet. When he explained, in his first week, why a transaction had failed, a man in Ohio with a frog for a face typed ser you are a legend, and Kieran sat in the blue light of the spare room with his heart going like a boy's.

In July he put in six thousand pounds: his half of a holiday fund the pandemic had cancelled. It was his money. It was not the deposit. He told himself that, and it was true.

He began to farm. That was the word. You put your seed into a field and it grew and you harvested it and replanted, and the fields were open all night, and the crop did not care what time it was in Wiltshire. He set alarms for three and five. He knew which pools would thin at which hours, the way his grandfather's grandfather must have known the weather over the downs. It did not occur to him until much later that he was not farming the tokens so much as they were farming him: that the thing he had planted was himself, and it was being harvested every night at three.

The yields were real, some of them. By Christmas his six thousand was fifteen. By February it was thirty-eight.

In September, when people were allowed to meet outdoors in sixes, Barbara emailed him from the address on her letter, which he had answered in March with a single line, and asked if he walked.

## Five: Tribe

They met in the car park above Avebury on a Sunday with the wind coming across the downs like something with a job to do. She was smaller than her voice, grey-haired, in a waxed jacket older than he was, with a collie that ignored him. She walked faster than he did.

They went up onto the Ridgeway, the chalk track white between the fields, and she told him about herself in the order she chose. A Rodbourne girl, her father a boilermaker in the works. An apprentice electrician there from 1969, one of three women in the whole place, and the men called her Babs and she answered only to Barbara. Seventeen years of it. Then March 1986, and the works closing after a hundred and forty years, and the town's clock stopping, and her thirty-three with a trade nobody wanted.

"So I learned computers," she said. "Nights at the tech. First in the exam. The bureau took me on in '88 and I spent twenty-five years keeping the clearing standing up. Nobody ever put my name on anything. That's fine. I know what I built."

"And now you don't trust it."

"I watched it nearly stop in 2008," she said. "The money waited and I didn't know if it would arrive. Then someone wrote nine pages describing a ledger nobody could switch off, and I read it three times on the train. I bought my first coins the year I retired. I never sell." She looked at him sideways. "What are you in? Not coins. Tell me you're not in the casino."

He told her about the Sharehouse, and the farms, and the fifteen thousand. She laughed, not kindly.

"It's the old casino in a new costume," she said. "You've swapped a bank you can't see for a hundred blokes with cartoon faces you can't see. At least the bank has a mural."

Below them the great stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and leaning, and she stopped to look at them the way you might look at an old colleague.

"Farmers put those there," she said. "Four and a half thousand years ago. Dragged them across the downs on sledges, stones of forty tons, generation after generation. Do you know why?"

"Religion?"

"To gather," she said. "People will do almost anything to have somewhere to gather. That chat room of yours is the same animal. Worse food."

He did not tell her that she was wrong, because she was not, exactly. He did not tell her either that the Sharehouse was the first band since the guild that needed him, or that when he typed and forty people answered he felt what the men in the back room of the White Hart must have felt: that he was one of them, and they were building something, and it would hold.

They walked most months after that. She never asked him to stop. She had never been anybody's mother and did not mean to start.

## Six: Harvest

In January 2021 the third lockdown sent Laura back onto the unit for the worst weeks of the whole thing. She came home in the dark and sat in the car outside the flat for twenty minutes before she came up, and Kieran watched her from the spare-room window and did not go down, because he thought she wanted to be alone, and because he did not know what he would say.

When she did come up she took her uniform off in the hallway and put it straight in the machine and stood in the shower until the hot water ran out, and later she lay on the sofa scrolling through houses in Wroughton on her phone. It was the only thing she looked at that was not a monitor. A house with a front door. A garden with a shed. She had been moved on from five rented houses before she was thirteen, and she had never once, in eight years with him, asked for anything except that.

The market went up. By February his six thousand was thirty-eight thousand pounds, then forty-five. The Sharehouse had grown to four thousand members. People asked for Kez by name. He answered newcomers at three in the morning with a patience he had once kept for a guild of forty, and they thanked him, and he felt like someone who mattered.

In June the Hart's board decided to block payments to several crypto exchanges, after the regulator's warnings and a run of scam losses, and because Kieran knew better than anyone on the floor why a payment failed, they seconded him to the project that built the block. He sat in meetings about it on Teams with his camera on and his lanyard round his neck. At night he walked round it through a payments app the Hart had never heard of. He did not feel he was lying. He felt, if anything, that he was the only person in both rooms.

At the end of July the car plant made its last car. His father worked the final shift and came home with his hi-vis vest and a commemorative mug and put the mug in a cupboard and did not take it out again. He was fifty-nine. He had built engines there since 1992.

For three months Kieran paid his parents' rent. He did it from his gains, by bank transfer, and his father thanked him once, in the car outside the Job Centre, without looking at him. It was the best thing the money ever did. It was also the thing that made him certain the money was real, that the game was not a game, that he had been right.

By November 2021 his screen said he was worth seventy-one thousand pounds. Laura had a shortlist of three houses. And three clocks were running in him at once, though he would not have put it like that: a body built for a band of faces on the downs, which wanted sleep and wanted to be told it was one of them; an employer built for the three-day cycle, which closed at five and remembered everything; and a phone that never closed at all, lighting the ceiling at three and five, calling him to the harvest.
