## Seven: Anchor

After November the prices went down, the way weather goes down, gradually and then all at once and then gradually again. By Christmas his seventy-one thousand was fifty; by the new year, forty-five. The Sharehouse stopped talking about moons and started talking about safe yield.

Safe yield was a dollar. Not a real dollar, but a token that was always worth a dollar, and a savings protocol that paid nearly twenty percent a year for holding it. The token did not keep its dollar with dollars in a vault. It kept it with a trick of arithmetic: anyone could always swap one of them for a dollar's worth of a sister token, and back, and the swapping held the price in place like a tent held by its guy ropes. Kieran read the paper. He understood it. He understood that the ropes held only while the sister token was worth more than all the dollars tied to it. It was a peg. Everything he had ever worked in was a peg, when you looked at it: the Hart's deposits, the three-day cycle, the plant, the works. They held because everyone believed they would hold.

House prices in Wroughton had gone up faster than the deposit. The deposit was twenty-four thousand pounds in a savings account at the Hart, earning nothing, and fifteen thousand of it was Laura's overtime from two pandemic winters.

On a Sunday in January he said it at the kitchen table. The rota was on the fridge behind her. She was marking dates on the calendar for the viewings.

"I want to move the deposit somewhere it'll earn something," he said. "Twenty percent. Before we buy in the summer. It's a dollar. It doesn't move."

She did not look up for long. She had worked seven nights out of the last ten.

"You're the one who knows about money," she said. "Do what you think."

He did not explain what a stablecoin was, and she did not ask. By the end of the week the twenty-four thousand pounds had left the Hart through the app it had never heard of and become twenty-four thousand pounds' worth of dollars that did not move, earning twenty percent, and he checked it every morning like a man checking a sleeping child.

In February he told Barbara on the Ridgeway, pleased, walking towards the long barrow. She stopped. She looked at the barrow for a long time, at the great stones of its door, and he waited for her to say the thing about casinos.

"That's yours and Laura's to decide," she said.

She said nothing else about it all afternoon. She talked about the men who dragged the stones and about the dog. On the drive home she thought of three sentences she could have said, one of them about guy ropes, and said none of them, because she had never been anybody's mother and did not mean to start.

## Eight: Hole

On Saturday 7 May the dollar that did not move moved. Ninety-nine cents. Then back. The Sharehouse made jokes about it. On Monday evening, the ninth, it was ninety-eight, then ninety-three, then eighty. Laura was on nights; the rota said so.

Kieran sat at the spare-room desk with the Sharehouse open on one screen and the price on the other, and he did not sleep, and the ropes went one by one.

He knew exactly what was happening. That was the worst of it. People were selling the dollar and swapping it for the sister token, and each swap made new sister tokens out of nothing, and each new sister token made the others worth less, so each swap needed more of them, and the fall fed itself like a fire in a draught. He could have drawn it on a whiteboard. He could have explained it to the fraud team in a short, excellent email.

The Sharehouse said: it has always come back. It said: do not sell, that is what they want. It said: we are all in this together, and a thousand cartoon faces said it with him. The founder's account said he was deploying more capital. On Tuesday it touched sixty cents and bounced to ninety and the Sharehouse cheered like a crowd at a match, and he held.

He could have sold at ninety. He could have sold at sixty. He did not, because selling would make it real, and because leaving the band while it was losing was the one thing he had never forgiven himself for, since the night at twenty when he had given the guild to Sander and logged off. The oldest part of him, the part that was older than the Hart and older than the stones, knew that the worst thing that could happen to a person was to be alone outside the fire. It did not know about percentages.

By Thursday it was twenty cents. By Friday the thirteenth the deposit was worth about three thousand pounds and falling. The sister token was worth almost nothing. The Sharehouse had gone from certainty to instructions to silence to jokes to goodbyes.

That same week the Hart's fraud lines filled with customers who had lost their savings, some of them through the same apps he had used to walk round the block he had built, and the payments team was pulled onto the phones. On Wednesday he took a call from a man in Stratton who had put in his redundancy money and wanted to know if the Hart could get it back. Kieran read him the script. His own voice came out of his mouth steady and kind and professional, the voice he had used on Barbara, and the man cried, and Kieran said he was very sorry, and he was, and he went home and checked a balance that was a number and then a smaller number.

## Nine: Silence

For five weeks he said nothing.

He went to work and came home. He took the calls. He checked the dead balance at night; by June it was a few hundred pounds. He lost weight. His hand shook when he held his phone, and he started holding it in the other hand. He slept three hours a night and told Laura it was the heat.

When the mortgage adviser emailed in the middle of June for their savings statements, Laura forwarded it to him with a thumbs-up and a house emoji.

He told her that evening at the kitchen table, with the rota on the fridge behind her. He told her everything, in the order it had happened: January, the dollar that did not move, the ropes, the Tuesday bounce, the Friday, the five weeks. He told her how much, to the pound. He did not cry, because he did not think he was allowed to.

She listened. She asked three questions. How much is left. Is any of it recoverable. Does anyone else know.

Then she went to bed.

In the morning she moved their accounts into her name, picked up two extra shifts a week, and cancelled the mortgage appointment. She did not shout. She did not cry in front of him. She had learned on five doorsteps, watching her mother hand back keys, that you hold everything together and say nothing, and she was very good at it, and she held.

The flat went quiet in a new way. Before, they had been two people with screens: hers the monitors, his the charts. Now there was one screen fewer and no more words. The rota stayed on the fridge. He read it every morning to know when she would be asleep, awake or gone, and understood for the first time that he had been reading it for two years as a timetable of when no one would be watching.

In July he walked with Barbara and told her everything too. She did not laugh, and she did not say anything about casinos. She walked a long way without speaking, faster than him, and at the long barrow she said, "I gave you a key and not a map," and then nothing else, and he thought that was the end of it.

## Ten: Stone

On a weekday in August, while Kieran was at the Hart, Barbara Ashby took the bus into Swindon, which she had not done in two years, and walked up Wood Street, and knocked on the door of a flat above a shop.

Laura answered in a hoodie, between nights. She did not know who this small grey woman was.

"I'm Barbara," the woman said. "I'm the one who sent him the card. Can I come in? I won't stay."

She did not sit down. She stood in the kitchen by the fridge with the rota on it, in her waxed jacket, and told Laura what she had done. She had given Kieran a key without a map. She had checked the key and not the person, which was the opposite of everything she believed. And in February, on the Ridgeway, when he told her where the deposit was, she had known exactly how the peg could fail, she could have drawn it for him, and she had said it was his choice and nothing else, because she had never been anybody's mother and had been very proud of that for sixty-nine years.

"That part's mine," she said. "The rest is his. I'm not here to make it better. I can't. I'm here because somebody in this should say it out loud to you, and it isn't going to be him, and it's apparently not going to be you." She looked at Laura properly for the first time. "You're allowed to say something. That's all. You're allowed."

When she had gone Laura sat at the kitchen table for a long time. Then she took the rota off the fridge, and looked at the months of it, the longs and the nights and the days, and put it back.

That evening she said it. She said it all. She said it about the five houses and the boxes she had never unpacked, and about the front door, and about what fifteen thousand pounds of pandemic nights meant in a body, and about the five weeks, which were worse than the money, much worse, and about January and "do what you think," which she knew she had said, and hated that she had said, and would not let him have all of, because she had said it, because she was so tired of being the one who held everything that she had handed it to him without looking.

He listened. It was the first conversation they had had in two years that neither of them was having through a screen.

## Eleven: Field, Again

They stayed. They chose it, which is different from staying.

They opened one account that both of them could see. They stuck a spreadsheet on the fridge next to the rota, held by the magnet from Weymouth. They postponed the house by two years. Kieran deleted the apps and told Laura every number, every time, until telling her every number stopped feeling like confession and started feeling like weather: something that came over the downs whether you minded or not, and that you talked about.

Laura, who had never asked for anything, asked for Sundays off together, and got them. She was astonished by how easy it was.

Rebuilding was slow and dull and they chose that too. It was harvest in the old sense: the same field, the same work, every season.

In November a big exchange collapsed in the background, and it turned out the customers' money had been used by its trading firm, and bitcoin fell to a quarter of its peak. Barbara's coins fell with it. She said she had seen three of these. She did not sell. On the Ridgeway she began, unasked, to explain things to him. How the clearing actually worked, file by file. Why the bit that waits had been written to wait. What a key was, and what a map was, and the difference. The walks became lessons, and he let them.

Half the Sharehouse's channels were empty now. A few regulars still posted gm in the mornings, from Ohio and Lisbon and Manila. Kieran looked in sometimes. He did not trade. Once he answered a newcomer at three in the morning who wanted to know if it was too late to get in, and he wrote a long careful message about pegs, and then deleted it, and wrote: ask someone who loves you to sit with you while you decide. Then he closed the laptop and went to bed, where Laura was asleep, on a day off, next to him.

## Twelve: Awake

He stayed on the payments-exceptions desk at the Hart. The routine was the same. He was not.

He knew how old the machine was now, and what ran under it, and who could switch it off. He knew what the new machine had done to him in the night. He kept the card in his wallet behind his bus pass, soft as cloth now, with its 0.0071 bitcoin still behind it, worth about a hundred and fifty pounds that spring; he had never spent it, and did not mean to. It was not a key any more. It was a reminder, kept by choice.

In March 2023 fraud rule forty-one stopped a payment of twelve thousand pounds from a saver in her twenties to a crypto exchange, and the case came into his queue, and he rang her.

He read her the script. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money. Have you been promised a return. Has anyone asked you to keep this private.

She said no to all of it, a little impatiently, the way he would have.

Then he said: "Can I ask you something that isn't on the script? Do you know how old the thing is that you're leaving? And how new the thing is that you're going into? And if it goes wrong, who are you going to tell?"

There was a silence on the line. It was not a hostile silence. He released the payment at 4.55, because nothing allowed him to stop it, and because it was hers, and he hoped she would tell someone.

On the Sunday the three of them walked on the Ridgeway: Barbara in front with the collie, too fast; Laura, who had never been up there, in the middle; Kieran behind. The chalk track ran white between the fields as it had for five thousand years. Below them the stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and leaning.

"Why would anyone drag them all that way?" Laura asked.

"To gather," Kieran said. "People will do almost anything to have somewhere to gather."

Barbara snorted, because it was her line, and did not mind.

His phone was in the car. The card was in his wallet. Three clocks were running in him still, and would be all his life: the body's, which wanted the fire and the faces; the institution's, which closed at five and waited three days; and the machine's, which never closed at all. He could hear all three. That was the difference. For most of his life he had heard only one, and thought it was the world.

The wind came over the downs. Laura took his hand without looking, the way you do when you are sure it will be there. They walked on towards the barrow, three small people on the oldest road in Britain, in the one corner of the map they had chosen, awake.
