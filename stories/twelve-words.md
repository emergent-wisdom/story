# Twelve Words

## One: Blue

The payments file left White Hart House at ten every night and came back at seven every morning with its failures sorted into a queue, and Kieran Hale had cleared that queue for nine years. Returned item, account closed. Name mismatch. Sort code retired in 2011. Fraud rule forty-one. He could read an exception the way his grandfather Ron, a fitter in the railway works until they shut, had read a boiler by the sound it made: not the words on the screen but the shape of what had gone wrong behind them.

The Hart had been founded in 1868 by railwaymen pooling their wages in the back room of a White Hart inn so that each of them, one by one, could buy a house. It still had the brass hart on the door and a mural of the founders in the canteen, men with moustaches and bowler hats looking faintly surprised to find themselves on a wall in west Swindon. Kieran liked the mural. He liked most things about the Hart. That was the problem, if it was a problem.

His week had one shape. The 8.12 bus from Old Town. The blue lanyard. The tea round, which went clockwise from the window and which nobody had ever agreed to and everybody kept. Lunch at one. The Thursday quiz at the Beehive, where his team came third nearly every week. Laura's rota on the fridge, printed monthly and held up by a magnet from Weymouth: days, nights, and the twelve-and-a-half-hour shifts she called longs. Sunday lunch in Penhill, where his father talked about the announced closure of the car plant in 2021 as if it were weather.

"They'll find something for the good ones," his father said, carving. "Pass the gravy."

"You're one of the good ones, Dad."

"Pass the gravy, Kieran."

He was twenty-nine. He was reliable. His appraisals used the word three times. At sixteen he had run a raiding guild of forty people across six countries in an online game, set the timetables, called the pulls, known who was sulking in Lisbon and who needed carrying in Tallinn, and nobody on the third floor knew it. When he took the job at twenty he had logged off for the last time, handed the guild to a Dutch boy called Sander, and become, deliberately, no trouble to anyone.

Some Sunday evenings the week sat on his chest like a cat. He kept it to himself. It seemed an ungrateful thing to feel in a town where people had queued outside the plant gates for jobs.

On Monday 21 October 2019, at 3.40 in the afternoon, fraud rule forty-one stopped a payment of forty thousand pounds from a savings account opened in 1989 to an account at a crypto exchange, and the case dropped into his queue with a small orange flag. Deb, from the next bank of desks, leaned round her monitor. She had sat next to him for six years and had decided long ago that he was the nicest man on the floor and slightly wasted on it.

"Ooh. Forty grand to bitcoin. That's a romance scam, that is. Some bloke in Ghana with a picture of a helicopter."

"Could be," Kieran said. "Probably not. Sorry, Deb, I just need to ring her."

He rang her.

"Hello, is that Mrs Ashby? Sorry to bother you. My name's Kieran, I'm calling from the White Hart's payments team about a transfer you made earlier today. It's nothing to worry about, it's just a check we do."

"Barbara," said the voice. "Never Mrs. You've got a script, haven't you. Go on, then. Read it to me."

He read her the script. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money. Have you been promised a return. Has anyone asked you to keep this conversation private. Have you been asked to download anything onto your computer.

She answered each question like a woman filling in a form she had designed herself. No. No. No. No.

"Brilliant, thank you, that's really helpful," he said, which was what you said. "I'll just make a note and then I can—"

"My turn," she said. "How old is the system you're sat in?"

"Sorry?"

"The clearing. Not your quick payments, the batch: the salaries, the direct debits, the thing that runs every night and takes three days to move money that could move in a second. How old is it?"

He found, with a small cold shock, that he did not know. "It's been updated a lot," he said. "I think."

"I updated it," said Barbara Ashby. "From 1988 to 2013, at a bureau in Reading. I wrote the bit that waits. It was built for magnetic tape, my love. It's got more patches on it than my dad's allotment shed. Do you know who could switch it off?"

"I'm not sure that's really—"

"No," she said. "You don't. Nobody on your floor does. That's not your fault. What's your surname, Kieran? And which desk is that?"

He told her: Hale, payments exceptions, third floor. Nothing in the script told him not to. He released the payment at 4.55, because nothing in the script allowed him to stop it, and he went home on the bus with the feeling of having walked past a door he had not known was in the wall.

## Two: Rabbit

The envelope came up from the post room on Friday 1 November, addressed in blue biro capitals: MR K. HALE, PAYMENTS EXCEPTIONS, THIRD FLOOR, WHITE HART HOUSE. It was the only letter anyone on the floor had received by name in a year, and Deb whistled.

"Is it a love letter? Kieran. Is it from the helicopter man?"

"It's a customer thing," he said. "It's fine. Honestly. It's just a thank-you, I think."

Inside was a white index card and a sheet of lined paper torn from a pad.

The card had twelve words on it in the same capitals, numbered, in two columns. They were ordinary words, some of them almost a poem, and he read them twice and then again, and they stayed words. The note said: There is fifty pounds behind these. It is yours. Go and look. B.A. On the back, in smaller capitals, was an email address.

He knew what the words were before he let himself know it. He had cleared enough payments to exchanges and read enough of the fraud team's bulletins. It was a recovery phrase: twelve words from a fixed list which were, in the new world, the whole of a wallet. Whoever held the words held the money. There was no branch behind them, no mural, no brass hart, no three-day cycle, no exceptions queue. Just the words.

Gifts from customers had to be declared. It was in the handbook, section nine, and in the refresher course Compliance ran every October in the training room, which he had sat a fortnight before: the one with the cartoon of a man holding a box of chocolates with a red cross over it. Declaring it would mean a form, and the form would mean Compliance, and Compliance would mean a note on Barbara Ashby's file. It would make her a case. It would make him a colleague who had accepted something.

In the guild there had been a rule, never written down, that you did not take loot you had not earned in front of people who had. He thought about that. Then he thought that there was nobody in front of him.

He put the card in his wallet, behind his bus pass, and did not declare it. He told nobody, not even Laura. It was the first thing in five years he had not told Laura, and all day he could feel it there behind the bus pass, a small square of weight.

He left the words alone. He looked them up once, on his phone in the third-floor toilets, confirmed what they were, and put the phone in his pocket and washed his hands. For four months the card rode in his wallet across the town and back. It softened at the corners. Every scam call he made after that had a second voice in it.

"Has anyone asked you to move your money?" he would say, and in his head Barbara said: How old is the thing you're sat in?

"Have you been promised a return?" And: Who could switch it off?

The routine stayed the same. It only began, faintly, to feel like a costume. Warm, well made, and somebody else's: the Hart fleece he wore on dress-down Fridays.

In the canteen the founders looked down from their mural with their moustaches and their surprise. He found himself wondering what the back room of the White Hart had smelt of in 1868, beer and coal smoke and wet wool, and whether any of those men had lain awake afraid that the thing they were building would not hold.

One Thursday Laura was off and came to the quiz. In the music round she sang the wrong words softly into his ear until he could no longer remember the right ones.

"Stop it. I knew this."

"Write mine," she said. "Mine's better."

"Lor."

She took the pencil and wrote the artist, who she had known all along, then pushed the sheet back. Under the table her cold hand found the gap between his cuff and his watch. He tried to shake her off without spilling his pint, and she laughed into his shoulder.

The question was which animal Alice followed down the hole, and everyone at the table wrote rabbit, and they came third.

## Three: Mirror

On the evening of Thursday 12 March 2020 the news said the markets had had their worst day since 1987 and bitcoin had lost half its value in a day. Laura was on a long and would not be home until after nine. The pasta bake she had left him sat under its foil on the side, with a note that said 40 MINS 180 LOVE YOU. She had written it at six that morning with her coat on, and had known, writing it, that he would forget. Kieran stood at the kitchen table with the television on in the other room and took the card out of his wallet.

He downloaded a wallet app. It asked him whether he wanted to create a new wallet or restore one. It felt like the character-select screen of a game he had not played for ten years: new, or continue. He pressed restore. It gave him twelve empty boxes, and he typed the words into them one by one.

Confirm, said the screen.

He put his thumb on the glass. For a moment nothing happened, and the black surface of the phone gave him back his own face, lit from below, a man in a kitchen in Old Town in a Hart fleece, tired, ordinary. Then the glass seemed to soften under his thumb, the way a puddle gives when you step in it expecting ground, and the balance rose out of nothing.

0.0071 BTC. £28.14.

Half of what she had given him. The first thing the other world taught him was that it moved while you watched it.

"Right," he said aloud, to nobody. "Okay. Right."

Then he found the explorer, the public ledger, and pasted in his address, and there was the transaction that had sent him the coins on 31 October 2019, and the one before that which had sent them to her, and behind that another, and another, branching back through thousands of hands and eleven years. There was no queue. There was no cut-off. There were no exceptions, only confirmations. Anyone could read it. Anyone in the world. He sat down on the kitchen chair so suddenly that it scraped.

It was like the moment in the old game when you rode out of the starting valley for the first time and the map, which you had thought was the world, turned out to be a green corner of a continent, with the rest of it black at the edges, waiting. New area discovered.

Laura came in at twenty past nine and found him still at the table with the pasta bake cold in its foil.

"You haven't eaten," she said. "Kieran. It said forty minutes. It was on the note."

"Sorry. Sorry. I got caught up. The markets are mad."

"Everything's mad." She put her bag down very carefully, as she did after a bad shift. "They're saying it's going to be us next. Like Italy. We're rehearsing proning tomorrow." She saw his face and stopped. "It's fine. Eat your tea."

On 23 March the country locked down. Laura went onto the COVID intensive-care unit at the hospital on the edge of town and began sleeping in the day with a towel over the curtain rail. Kieran carried the queue home on a laptop and set it up in the spare room, and the Hart shrank to a screen, and the week lost its shape. What it had instead was the laptop, and the phone, and the card, and every night after the file went, the black edges of the map.

He only read. He read until three in the morning, and in the day he cleared the exceptions queue from the spare room with Laura asleep through the wall. When she woke they talked about the car park and the canteen and whether the Co-op had flour, because there was nothing either of them could say about their real day that would not frighten the other.

What she did not tell him about were nights like the one in the middle of April, when the unit was running at half again as many beds as it was built for, and the alarms went all night like birds in a hedge. She spent twelve and a half hours in a hood that made her own breath loud in her ears. At three in the morning she and six others turned a man of fifty-eight onto his front, on the count of the one holding his head, every line and tube gathered in their hands so that nothing pulled, because on his front his lungs might take the air the machine was pushing into them. At five she held a phone to another man's ear so that his daughter in Leeds could tell him the cat was fine, and that she loved him, and that he was not to worry about the cat. She did not cry. You did not, on the unit. You did it in the car, or not at all.

At eight she came home and found Kieran asleep at the spare-room desk with his cheek on his arm and his phone lit beside him, a green line climbing on it. She stood in the doorway in her coat and thought: at least one of us is having an easy war. Then she took her uniform off in the hall, put the towel over the curtain rail and went to bed, and she did not tell him that either.

## Four: Field

What happened that summer happened to everyone who was there, and they all said the same thing about it afterwards, and the thing they said was: what the actual hell was that.

In the middle of June a lending protocol started paying its users in a new token just for using it, and within two months there were pools, and pools of pools, and tokens named after food, and people moving money between them at three in the morning to chase yields of four hundred, nine hundred, two thousand percent a year. Kieran read the words and none of them meant anything. Liquidity. Impermanent loss. Governance. Gas. Staking. Slippage. Rug. APY, APR, TVL, LP, DAO, ape, degen, wen, ser. A new word every hour, a new protocol every day, and every protocol named after something you could eat. A sushi one. A pasta one. A pickle one. A yam, which lasted about a day and a half and then broke, and everybody laughed about it, and somebody said they had lost their house.

"Oh my God," he said to the empty spare room at two in the morning, and then, because it was two in the morning, he typed into the search bar: wtf is impermanent loss.

He learned it anyway, the way he had learned the game: by doing the stupid thing first and reading the forums after.

In July he put in six thousand pounds: his own savings, which were going to pay for Crete, Laura's first holiday abroad since a school trip to Calais, before the pandemic cancelled it. It was his money. It was not the deposit. He told himself that on the landing, with his hand on the bathroom door, and it was true.

He began to farm. That was the word. You put your seed into a field and it grew and you harvested it and replanted, and the fields were open all night, and the crop did not care what time it was in Wiltshire. He set alarms for three and five, and learned which pools would thin at which hours. There were three clocks in the spare room now: his body's, which wanted to sleep at eleven; the Hart's, which sent the file at ten and brought it back at seven; and the pools', which never stopped. He started living by the third.

In August, the week the yam broke, a link in a thread about it took him to a chat server called the Sharehouse, where a few thousand anonymous people with cartoon avatars were awake in every time zone at once.

anyone awake, said the channel, at 3.14 a.m. British Summer Time.

awake ser

frens who is farming the new pool

ser the new pool is a rug

ser everything is a rug if you zoom out enough

He asked why a swap he was thinking of making would fail, and a man in Ohio with a frog for a face answered it in forty seconds, and a woman in Manila with a cat in sunglasses corrected the man in Ohio, and they argued, and in the end somebody called Dmitri with no avatar at all posted a diagram. Kieran understood the diagram. He understood it as he had understood raid bosses at fifteen: completely, and then in his sleep. The next week somebody new asked the same question, and he answered it before anyone else could.

ser you are a legend, typed the frog. who is this guy

kez, typed Dmitri. kez knows.

He sat in the blue light of the spare room with his heart going like a boy's.

The yields were real, some of them. By September his six thousand was nine.

"You're up early," Laura said once, coming in from a night at seven and finding him at the laptop in his dressing gown.

"Couldn't sleep," he said, and closed the lid, not quickly, because quickly would have meant something.

In March he had written to the address on the back of her note, a single line: thank you, I looked. In September, the month of the rule of six, she answered: Good. Do you walk? She had watched the coins on the public ledger all summer, and they had not moved.

## Five: Tribe

They met in the car park above Avebury on a Sunday, with a hard wind coming across the downs. She was smaller than her voice, grey-haired, in a waxed jacket older than he was, with a collie that ignored him completely. She kept her hands in her pockets. She looked him up and down the way you look at a fuse board.

"You're taller than you sound," she said. "Come on. She needs walking, and so do you, by the look of you."

She lived at Avebury Trusloe, a short walk west of the ring, in a cottage with a satellite dish she had put up herself.

She walked faster than he did. They went up onto the Ridgeway, the chalk track white between the fields, and she told him about herself in the order she chose, in short sentences.

"Rodbourne. Dad was a boilermaker in the works, Grandad before him. I wanted to be an electrician from when I was ten. Mended the wireless with a hairgrip. Nobody took it serious." A stile; she went over it without using her hands. "1969 they let me in as an apprentice. New manager. I beat every boy in the test. Three women on the tools in the whole works. The men called me Babs. I didn't answer to it. After about a year they stopped."

"My grandad was in the works," Kieran said. "Ron Hale. He was a fitter." He still had Ron's brass tally token, in a drawer at home.

She stopped on the track. "Ron Hale. Boiler shop. Big hands. Whistled Elvis." For a moment she looked at him differently. "He called me Barbara from the first week. Him and about two others." She set off again. "He was all right, your grandad."

"It must have been hard, though. The rest of them."

"It was a job. I was good at it. Seventeen years." She walked on. "Then March '86, and they shut the lot. Hundred and forty years, and the hooter just stops. Whole town's clock, that hooter. People who never set foot in the works set their kitchen clocks by it. And then it doesn't go, and you're thirty-two with a trade nobody wants."

"What did you do?"

"Learned computers. Nights at the tech. First in the exam." She said it flat, a meter reading, without pride. "The bureau took me on in '88. Twenty-five years keeping the clearing standing up. Nobody ever put my name on anything. That's fine. I know what I built. I know where the bodges are, too."

"And now you don't trust it."

"I watched it nearly stop in 2008." She slowed. "Money going out one end, not coming in the other, the whole thing holding its breath for a weekend. My code, waiting. I didn't know if it'd arrive. Then someone wrote nine pages about a ledger nobody can switch off, and I read it three times on the train to Reading. Bought my first coins the year I retired. Never sold one." She turned her head. "What are you in? Not coins. Don't tell me you're in the casino."

He told her about the Sharehouse, the pools, the farms, the nine thousand. She laughed: one short bark, not unkind, not kind either.

"Right," she said. "So you've swapped a bank you can't see for a hundred blokes with cartoon faces you can't see. At least the bank's got a mural."

"It's not like that. They're really good, some of them. There's this guy Dmitri, he—"

"Mind," she said, and stopped, and pointed.

Far below them the great stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and small at that distance, though some of them were taller than a double-decker, with the village inside the ring, pub and all.

"Farmers put those there," she said. "Four and a half thousand years ago. Dragged them across the downs on sledges. Forty tons, some of them. Took generations. Do you know why?"

"Religion?"

"That's what the signs say. I'll tell you what I think, for nothing." She pulled the collie's ear. "They wanted somewhere to gather. People will do almost anything to have a place to gather. That chat room of yours is the same animal. Worse food."

"I heard a man on the radio," she said. "Biologist. Ants, mostly. He said the trouble with people is we've got Palaeolithic emotions, medieval institutions and god-like technology." She looked down at the stones. "He missed one out. A phone in your hand at three in the morning."

Kieran thought that a phone in your hand at three in the morning was not a fourth thing but all three at once, and that the man on the radio had missed nothing. He let it stand. He kept the rest to himself as well: that when he typed and forty people answered he felt what the men in the back room of the White Hart must have felt: that he was one of them, that they were building something, that it would hold.

At the car park she opened her boot and the collie jumped in.

"Same time next month," she said. It was not a question. "Wear proper shoes."

They walked most months after that. She never once asked him to stop.

## Six: Harvest

In January 2021 the third lockdown sent Laura back onto the unit for the worst weeks of the whole thing. She came home in the dark and sat in the car outside the flat for twenty minutes before she came up. Kieran watched her from the spare-room window, the dome light off, her hands on the wheel, and stayed where he was, because he thought she wanted to be alone, and because he did not know what he would say if he did.

When she came up she took her uniform off in the hallway and put it straight into the machine on a sixty, and stood in the shower until the hot water ran out.

"How was it?" he said, when she came out.

"We lost two." She was towelling her hair, not looking at him. "One was forty-one. Sats wouldn't come up. We proned him four times." She hung the towel on the radiator, square. She hung everything square. "It's fine. What did you do?"

"Nothing. Work. The batch." He had made nine thousand pounds that week. He left it unsaid. It would have sounded like a different language, and it was.

Later she lay on the sofa scrolling through houses in Wroughton on her phone. It was the only thing she looked at that was not a monitor. A three-bed semi with a front door painted green. A garden with a shed.

"That one's got a porch," she said. "Look. You could put your boots in it."

"It's nice."

"It's got a front door that's ours." She said it lightly. She said all the important things lightly, so they could be taken as jokes if they had to be. She had been moved on from five rented houses before she was thirteen. In six years with him she had never once asked for anything except that.

The market went up. By the Christmas just gone his six thousand had been fifteen thousand pounds; by February it was thirty-eight; then forty-five. The Sharehouse had grown to four thousand members. People asked for Kez by name.

kez is this safe

kez wen new pool

kez my mum wants to get in, what do i tell her

tell her to only put in what she can lose, he wrote, and felt wise, and did not think about his own mother.

In June the Hart's board decided to block payments to several crypto exchanges, after the regulator's warnings and a run of scam losses, and because Kieran knew better than anyone on the floor why a payment failed, they seconded him to the project that built the block.

It was the year everybody was at war with everybody. The regulator said anyone buying crypto should be prepared to lose all their money, and the Sharehouse printed it on a T-shirt. Banks blocked exchanges, exchanges bought the fronts of football shirts, and the Sharehouse posted a tyrannosaurus in a tie with the caption have fun staying poor.

The block went live on a Thursday in July. At nine there was a call on Teams to mark it, nine faces in nine spare rooms, and Harriet from Compliance, who was new and very serious, shared her screen: a bar chart of the year's scam losses, taller every month. She told them about a widow in Highworth who had sent her savings to a man she had met on an app. "We're seeing a lot of harm," she said. "Kieran, you know what that looks like better than any of us."

His phone lay face down by his keyboard and buzzed all through the call. It was #war-room, where a sixth-former in Hull was explaining that the Hart and everything like it were legacy institutions and deserved what was coming.

"I think if we just look at the merchant category codes we'll catch most of it," Kieran said, and his project manager said, "Brilliant, Kieran, that's exactly the kind of thing."

That night a new pool opened at midnight, and he wanted two thousand pounds in it before the yield came down. His card was refused. He sat and looked at the refusal for a while, the reason code and the short polite sentence under it, which he had helped to write. Then he opened a payments app the Hart had never heard of, sent the money to himself, and from himself to the exchange, and it arrived in forty seconds. It never felt like lying.

At the end of July the car plant made its last car. His father worked the final shift and came home with his hi-vis vest and a commemorative mug and put the mug in the cupboard over the kettle and did not take it out again.

"They gave us a mug," he said at Sunday lunch.

"Dad. I'm so sorry."

"It's only a mug." He carved. "Pass the gravy."

For three months, until the union won back his father's agency years and the redundancy money came through, Kieran paid his parents' rent from his gains, by bank transfer on the first of the month, with the reference MUM AND DAD. His father thanked him once, in the car outside the Job Centre, without looking at him.

"You didn't have to."

"I wanted to."

"Well." His father looked at the Job Centre's glass door, and at his own reflection in it. "Your grandad would have liked that. Doesn't matter where it comes from, he used to say, as long as it comes."

It was the best thing the money ever did. After that he could not hear anyone call it a game without thinking of the first of the month.

His mother asked him once, in the kitchen with the door shut, whether it was that bitcoin. He said it was savings. She said, "Well, be careful," and he said he was.

In October the Sharehouse met in Swindon. One of the regulars, a cartoon owl, turned out to live in Stratton, and she booked the back room of a pub in Old Town in the name of a darts team. You went past the toilets and through a door marked PRIVATE FUNCTION, and there it was, the hidden world, in a room that smelt of carpet and chips. Eleven of them. The owl was a dental nurse called Mandy. There was a lorry driver who had made four hundred thousand pounds and still drove the lorry, because what else would he do all day. There was a retired postman, a man from the council's parking department, and a boy who should not have been in a pub and whose mother thought he was at chess club. Nobody looked like their avatar. Nobody looked like a wizard. They looked like the queue at the post office.

"You're Kez," Mandy said, and held out her hand. "I thought you'd be older."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. You saved me eleven hundred quid in the summer with that thing about slippage." She lifted her glass. "To Kez."

"To Kez," said the room. He lifted his pint too, then saw what he was doing and lowered it. Mandy made room for him on the bench. The lorry driver leaned across the table to ask him something, and used his name.

By November 2021 his screen said he was worth seventy-one thousand pounds. He liked it, more than he would have admitted. Some nights he opened the app only to look at the number, and felt it in his shoulders like a warm bath. His alarms went at three and five. The rota on the fridge said LONG, LONG, OFF, NIGHT, NIGHT, NIGHT, and he read it every morning as he read the price: to know what was coming.

Laura had a shortlist of three houses and a folder called HOUSE on the old laptop they shared. He had never opened it. One night that month, with Laura asleep through the wall between two nights, he opened the old laptop to pay the electricity, and there it was on the desktop where it had been for a year, and he opened it.

Three houses, each in its own folder, the listings saved. A spreadsheet of what the mortgage would be at four different rates. A document called DEPOSIT with one line in it, updated in September: 21,200. Under it, in her capitals: 3,800 TO GO.

He sat with his phone in his other hand and the number on it. He could sell in the morning and pay the deposit into the joint account three times over, and she would never do another extra night for it. He had the app open. What stopped him was not the price. It was the question she would ask, which was where it had come from, and the answer, which was a year and a half of nights she had slept through and a card behind his bus pass she had never heard of.

So he told himself the thing he went on telling himself all winter: not yet. At a hundred thousand it would be a surprise, and a surprise is not a secret.

He closed the folder the way she had left it, and paid the electricity, and went back to the spare room.

## Seven: Anchor

After November the prices went down, gradually and then all at once and then gradually again. By Christmas his seventy-one thousand was fifty. By the new year it was forty-five. His own money stayed where it was, in the farm tokens and the pools, because moving it would have meant selling at the bottom, and he did not sell at the bottom. The Sharehouse stopped talking about moons.

zoom out

it's a healthy correction ser

anyone who sells here deserves to be poor

smart money is going to safe yield, typed Dmitri. twenty percent on a dollar. it literally does not move.

Safe yield was a dollar. Not a real dollar, but a token that was always worth a dollar, and a savings protocol that paid nearly twenty percent a year for holding it. The token did not keep its dollar with dollars in a vault. It kept it with a trick of arithmetic: anyone could always swap one of them for a dollar's worth of a sister token, and back, and all that swapping held the price in place like a tent held by its guy ropes. Kieran read the paper. He understood it. He understood that the ropes held only while the sister token was worth more than all the dollars tied to it, and he understood that everybody in the tent believed it would hold, and that the believing was part of what held it. It was a peg. Everything he had ever worked in was a peg, when you looked: the Hart's deposits, the three-day cycle, the plant, the works. They held because everyone agreed they would.

House prices in Wroughton had gone up faster than the deposit. The deposit was twenty-four thousand pounds in a savings account at the Hart, earning nothing, and fifteen thousand of it was Laura's, most of it overtime from two pandemic winters.

On the Saturday night he ran the script on himself, the way he ran it on strangers. Has anyone contacted you and asked you to move your money? Nobody had asked him; he had found it himself, which was different. Have you been promised a return? Not a return: a rate, on a dollar. Has anyone asked you to keep this conversation private? Only until it had grown; at a hundred thousand it would have been a surprise. Have you been asked to download anything onto your computer? He had helped write the block. He knew what the rules would not see. He heard each answer go past the point at which he would have kept a customer on the line, and he did not ring himself back. He held the payment until 4.55, as long as the day would let him, and then released it, because nothing in the rules let him stop it, and because it was hers. He hoped she would ring her brother first.

For a week afterwards he half expected someone from Quality to come down to the third floor with a transcript. Nobody did.

On the Sunday the three of them walked on the Ridgeway: Barbara in front with the collie, too fast; Laura, who had never been up there, in the middle, in new boots; Kieran behind. The chalk track ran white between the fields, as it had for five thousand years. Far below them the stones stood in their circle round the village, grey and small, with smoke going up from the pub chimney.

"Why would anyone drag them all that way?" Laura asked.

"To gather," Kieran said. "People will do almost anything to have a place to gather."

Barbara snorted, because it was her line. "He steals everything," she said to Laura. "Watch your purse."

"I know," Laura said. "He's got my deposit."

She had not made a joke about it before. Barbara laughed, and Kieran laughed too, a beat late.

His phone was in the car.

The wind came over the downs. Laura took his hand without looking. She was sure it would be there.
